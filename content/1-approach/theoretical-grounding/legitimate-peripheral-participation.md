For where this shows up concretely in the curriculum, see Chapter 3's cornerstone projects — real, ongoing software systems students join and take on progressively larger roles in over the two-year core.*

## What legitimate peripheral participation is

Lave and Wenger's central claim, developed from ethnographic studies of apprenticeship — Yucatec Mayan midwives, tailors in Vai and Gola communities, U.S. Navy quartermasters, meat cutters, and members of Alcoholics Anonymous — is that learning is not primarily an individual, cognitive process of absorbing decontextualized knowledge ([Lave & Wenger, 1991](../../7-references#lave-wenger-1991)). It is a process of increasing participation in a **community of practice**: a group of people bound together by a shared activity they are actually doing.

A newcomer starts at the *periphery* — doing real, low-stakes-but-genuine work (not a simulation or a graded exercise divorced from the practice) that is still recognized by the community as legitimate. As competence and trust grow, the newcomer takes on more central, higher-stakes tasks, and their identity shifts from outsider to full practitioner. The learning and the identity change are the same process, not two separate things — you don't first learn to be a tailor and then join the tailors; you become a tailor by progressively doing more of what tailors do, alongside tailors.

This is a sharp contrast with a curriculum that treats a discipline as a body of content to transmit, then tests for retention afterward. Under legitimate peripheral participation (LPP), the practice comes first; the content is learned in service of participating in it.

## What makes a community of practice, specifically

Lave and Wenger's original 1991 book introduces the concept through case studies rather than a checklist. Wenger's later, solo elaboration gives the operational criteria used since ([Wenger, 1998](../../7-references#wenger-1998)): a community of practice exists where three elements are jointly present, not any one alone —

- **Mutual engagement** — members actually interact and coordinate around a shared activity, not just share a label or credential.
- **Joint enterprise** — the group is accountable to a shared goal it has negotiated together, not one handed down without buy-in.
- **Shared repertoire** — the group accumulates its own concepts, tools, jargon, war stories, and routines over time, which newcomers learn by using, not by being handed a glossary.

A classroom that merely groups students to work in parallel on individually-graded assignments does not meet this bar — there is no joint enterprise and no accumulating shared repertoire. A studio or project team that owns a real, evolving artifact and is jointly accountable for it is much closer to the model.

## The classroom translation: cognitive apprenticeship

Lave and Wenger's cases are workplace apprenticeships, not classrooms, which raises an immediate question for a curriculum design: what does "legitimate peripheral participation" mean when the setting is a course, not a trade? Collins, Brown, and Newman's cognitive apprenticeship framework is the standard answer, translating the apprenticeship structure into concrete instructional moves for cognitive (not just manual) skills ([Collins et al., 1989](../../7-references#collins-1989)): an expert models a task while making their normally-invisible thinking visible, the novice attempts it under coaching and scaffolding that fades as competence grows, and the novice periodically articulates and reflects on their own process rather than only producing output. This is the mechanism by which a bounded, gradable course activity can still function as genuine peripheral participation rather than an isolated exercise.

## Why this fits a cornerstone-project curriculum

A curriculum organized around real, ongoing software systems — rather than only disposable toy assignments — is a direct structural bet on LPP. A first-semester student's contribution to a cornerstone project (see Chapter 3) can be genuinely peripheral: small, bounded, closely coached, but still a real change to a real system that other students and the system's actual stakeholders depend on — not a simulation of the work. As a student advances through the two-year core, their role in the same project (or a more advanced one) can move toward the center: larger scope, less coaching, more accountability for decisions other students will build on. This is the same trajectory Lave and Wenger describe, and it is also why cornerstone projects and the spiral curriculum (see [Spiral Curriculum](./spiral-curriculum)) reinforce each other: a spiral revisits the *same concepts* with increasing formal rigor; LPP revisits the *same practice* with increasing centrality and stakes. They are two lenses on the same underlying design move — deliberate, structured return, not a single pass.

## What the evidence actually supports

Lave and Wenger's book is an interpretive, ethnographic argument, not a controlled study — it is best read as a powerful *descriptive and analytic* account of how apprenticeship learning actually works, not as a causal claim that has been tested against an alternative model the way a curricular intervention would be. Its influence has been enormous (the terms "community of practice" and "legitimate peripheral participation" are now standard vocabulary well beyond education, especially in workplace and organizational learning research), but that influence is a measure of the framework's explanatory reach, not by itself evidence that designing a course around it improves measurable learning outcomes.

The more direct, testable evidence sits one level down, in the cognitive apprenticeship literature that operationalizes these ideas into specific classroom techniques (modeling, coaching, scaffolding with fading, articulation, reflection) — this is the same evidentiary pattern as the spiral curriculum page: a strong, well-argued theoretical case, with the more rigorous causal evidence living in the *adjacent, more concrete* instructional mechanisms rather than in tests of the overarching framework itself. A dedicated evidence pass — parallel to the one run for the spiral curriculum page — is the right next step before this page's claims are treated as fully load-bearing; it has not been done yet for this page.

## What evidence-centered design is

Evidence-centered design (ECD) reframes assessment design as building an explicit **evidentiary argument**, not writing a set of test items ([Mislevy et al., 2003](../../7-references#mislevy-steinberg-almond-2003)). The claim you want to make about a student — "this student can reason about concurrency," "this student can design a normalized schema" — is not directly observable. An assessment is only useful to the extent that a specific, designed-in-advance chain of reasoning connects something you *can* observe (a work product, a performance, an answer) back to that unobservable claim. ECD's core discipline is to design that chain deliberately, before writing a single task, rather than writing tasks first and hoping they happen to support the claims you later want to make.

This inverts the usual order of assessment design. The starting question is not "what should we ask students to do?" but "what claim do we want to make about the student, and what evidence would actually justify that claim?" Tasks are then engineered specifically to elicit that evidence — a task that doesn't produce evidence bearing on a real claim is not doing assessment work, however elaborate it looks.

## The three models

Mislevy, Steinberg, and Almond formalize the evidentiary chain as three interlocking models, each with a specific job:

- **The student model** — the claims themselves: the set of knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs) the assessment is designed to support inferences about. This is the "what are we actually trying to find out" layer.
- **The evidence model** — the rules connecting a student's observable work to the student model. It has two parts: how do you identify and score the salient features of a work product (an evaluation rule), and how does that score update belief about the student-model claims (a measurement rule)? This is the layer that makes the inference *rigorous* rather than impressionistic.
- **The task model** — the specification of the situation presented to the student: what materials, prompts, constraints, and affordances are given, and what form the resulting work product takes. This is the layer that actually elicits the evidence the evidence model needs.

A later elaboration extends this into a five-layer process — domain analysis, domain modeling, the conceptual assessment framework (the three models above), assessment implementation, and assessment delivery — for scaling the same discipline from a single task up to an operational testing program ([Mislevy et al., 2003](../../7-references#mislevy-almond-lukas-2003); [Mislevy & Haertel, 2006](../../7-references#mislevy-haertel-2006)). The common thread across every layer is the same: nothing is built until it's traceable back to a specific claim and a specific piece of evidence that would support or undercut it.

## Why this fits a competency-based curriculum

A program that separates competency status from course grades — as this one does — cannot get away with informal, after-the-fact assessment design. If a competency claim ("this student has attained Computational Reasoning") is going to be certified across many different courses, each potentially using different tasks, the programmatic-level integrity of that claim depends entirely on whether every course's tasks were actually designed to produce real evidence for it, not just designed to look topically related. ECD is the discipline that keeps that honest: every signature assessment and recurring assessment type in Chapter 5 is implicitly a task model, every competency in the competency model is a student-model claim, and every rubric or scoring guide connecting the two is an evidence model. Naming this explicitly is what lets the program audit itself — is there an actual evidentiary chain from this task to this competency claim, or just a plausible-sounding label?

This also gives a concrete, checkable failure mode to watch for as courses are built out: a task that produces a grade but no clear evidence for any specific competency claim is not doing programmatic assessment work, however legitimate it is as a course requirement.

## What the evidence actually supports

ECD is a design methodology and a quality-assurance discipline, not itself a causal intervention to be tested against a control group — the relevant question is not "does ECD raise test scores" but "does building an assessment this way produce a more defensible validity argument than building one without this discipline." On that question, ECD's track record is real but institutional rather than experimental: it was developed at Educational Testing Service and has been the explicit design basis for large operational assessment and simulation-based testing programs (Cisco Networking Academy's assessments and NetPASS, intelligent-tutoring and diagnostic systems like ACED, and components of NAEP), which is evidence of adoption and perceived design rigor by testing organizations with strong incentives to get validity right, not a controlled comparison against alternative design methods.

This is the same evidentiary shape as the other pages in this chapter: a strong, well-specified design framework with real institutional adoption, whose main empirical backing is in the broader validity-argument literature it draws from (educational measurement and validity theory) rather than in head-to-head outcome studies of "ECD-designed assessments" versus otherwise-designed ones.

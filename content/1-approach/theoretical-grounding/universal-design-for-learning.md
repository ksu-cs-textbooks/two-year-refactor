> *Working draft for faculty review. This page describes the general research basis for designing course materials with built-in flexibility rather than retrofitted accommodations.*

## What Universal Design for Learning is

Universal Design for Learning (UDL) applies an idea from architecture — design a building's entrances and spaces to work for the widest range of bodies from the start, rather than building for one "standard" body and bolting on a wheelchair ramp later — to instructional design. CAST's foundational articulation frames the target of instruction as the "expert learner," and its central premise is that learner variability is the norm, not the exception: a course designed for one imagined average student will systematically underserve everyone who doesn't match that profile, whereas a course built with multiple ways in from the outset serves a much wider range of learners without requiring an individual accommodation request for each one ([Rose & Meyer, 2002](../../7-references#rose-meyer-2002)). The current operational version of the framework is maintained and periodically revised by CAST ([CAST, 2024](../../7-references#cast-2024)).

## The three principles, operationalized

UDL organizes its guidance around three principles, each targeting a different part of the learning process:

- **Multiple means of engagement** — the *why* of learning: options for recruiting interest, sustaining effort and persistence through a task, and developing self-regulation. Answers "why would a student stay motivated through this."
- **Multiple means of representation** — the *what* of learning: options for perception, for language and symbols, and for comprehension. Answers "how does the material actually reach a student whose default way of taking in information doesn't fit the way it was presented."
- **Multiple means of action and expression** — the *how* of learning: options for physical action, for expression and communication, and for executive function (planning and monitoring one's own work). Answers "how does a student show what they know" — a written exam is one option among several, not the only legitimate one.

The operative design move under all three is the same: build the alternative pathways into the course from the start, rather than treating "let me get you the material a different way" as a special-case accommodation triggered only after a student self-identifies a need.

## Where this fits a CS core — and where the fit is thinner

The most CS-specific applied work in this space comes from K-12: the UDL4CS research-practice partnership (University of Florida, CSTA, and district partners) maintains an actively updated set of UDL guidelines mapped specifically to CS teaching strategies, and NYC's CS4All initiative publishes a comparable practical resource. Both are useful as concrete examples of what a UDL principle looks like translated into an actual CS lesson, but both target K-12 classrooms, not an undergraduate, competency-based core — a translation gap worth naming explicitly rather than assuming K-12 exemplars carry over unchanged to adult learners in this program.

A more directly transferable piece of guidance is implementation-level rather than content-level: Israel, Lash, and Ray's practitioner guidance argues for starting with one or two UDL checkpoints per unit rather than attempting the full framework everywhere at once ([Israel et al., 2017](../../7-references#israel-2017)). That maps well onto this curriculum's own phased, page-by-page authoring process (this site is itself being built one chapter at a time) — UDL adoption across the two-year core should be expected to follow the same incremental pattern, not arrive fully formed in every course on day one.

## What the evidence actually supports

UDL's three principles are grounded in a real evidence base at the level of the underlying cognitive and affective mechanisms they draw on (multiple means of representation, for instance, overlaps directly with dual-coding and multimedia-learning research), and the framework has seen wide adoption and continuous institutional revision — CAST has iterated the guidelines through multiple major versions, most recently version 3.0. That adoption and iteration is evidence of practitioner and organizational confidence in the framework, not the same thing as controlled evidence that adopting UDL, as a whole design framework, causally improves measured learning outcomes in a given course. UDL is broad by design — three principles, nine guidelines, and many possible concrete instantiations of each — which makes it inherently difficult to test as a single intervention the way a specific technique can be tested; a course can honestly claim to "use UDL" while making very different concrete choices from another course that also claims to.

This is the same evidentiary shape as the other pages in this chapter: a well-specified, widely-adopted design framework whose supporting evidence is strongest at the level of its component mechanisms and weakest at the level of "the framework as a whole, causally."

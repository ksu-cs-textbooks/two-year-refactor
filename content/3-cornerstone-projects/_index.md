Real, ongoing software systems and institutional assets used throughout the curriculum as exemplars and living labs, in place of invented examples and disposable one-off assignments.

Some of these are systems the department builds and operates, with students as their developers — a long-lived, production codebase with real users, extended and maintained across cohorts rather than discarded at the end of a semester. Others are established, external infrastructure — real institutions with their own priorities and constraints — that students learn to integrate with rather than build themselves. Both kinds are deliberate: a curriculum that only ever gives students systems built for their own benefit never teaches them what it's like to depend on, or be depended on by, something outside their control.

Student involvement in a built system deepens as students advance through the program — genuinely peripheral, low-stakes work early on, moving toward real ownership of a system's evolution — the same trajectory described in [Legitimate Peripheral Participation](../1-approach/theoretical-grounding/legitimate-peripheral-participation), and a live instance of the [Spiral Curriculum](../1-approach/theoretical-grounding/spiral-curriculum)'s design logic: the same system revisited at increasing depth, not a repeat of the first pass. Where a project means working with an external stakeholder's real constraints, it's also a live instance of [Boundary Crossing](../1-approach/theoretical-grounding/boundary-crossing).

The projects are also deliberately architecturally diverse. Each takes a different real approach to the same classes of problems — networking, APIs, data at scale — so students see multiple legitimate answers by design, not one canonical toolchain presented as the only way.
